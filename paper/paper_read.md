# PoreBoostGAN
## PART1
Super-resolution technology aims to create mapping relationships between low-resolution and high-resolution images to reconstruct high-resolution images from low-resolution ones, offering broad application prospects. In geology, rock CT scans at different resolutions show vastly different characteristics. Low-resolution images preserve low-frequency information (contour information), while high-resolution imaging techniques retain more high-frequency information (details). However, high-resolution imaging hasn't solved all problems, as it requires the lens to be closer to the object, limiting high-resolution imaging to small fields of view. Super-resolution technology provides an ingenious solution to this problem - by training the mapping relationship between low and high-resolution images, we can map low-resolution wide-field images to obtain high-resolution wide-field images, something currently impossible with any instrument but easily achievable through super-resolution technology.

![image1](./figures/Fig1.png)

To validate this hypothesis, we used the carbonate rock dataset (MRCCM) from the University of New South Wales and BasicSR to implement this technology. 3D micro-CT is essentially the stacking of numerous 2D images along the z-axis. The resolution in the XY plane must match the z-axis resolution for physical significance (z-axis resolution refers to the distance between 2D images; 2D grayscale images can be stored as 2D arrays with values ranging from 0-255, efficiently stored in int8 format; 3D micro-CT is essentially a 3D array, commonly saved in TIFF format). As seen in subplots a and b of Figure 1, low-resolution images appear blurry at their resolution limit, often appearing gray because when machines can't determine if a position should be black (0) or white (255), they assign intermediate gray values. High-resolution images can capture more information at the same position with multiple points available for value assignment, naturally achieving higher resolution, but the same issue recurs when scaling these images to their resolution limit. While physical subdivision can continue indefinitely, images with resolution cannot - this is the contradiction between resolution and field of view.!![image2](./figures/Fig2.png)
Next, we'll discuss the 3D super-resolution experiment. As mentioned, 3D images are essentially stacked 2D images. We can create paired datasets needed for supervised learning by sampling and cutting 3D digital cores into 2D images. Notably, when obtaining high-resolution 2D images, we need to skip images across resolution multiples on the z-axis - for example, with 4x resolution, low-resolution images are cut normally, while high-resolution images need sampling every fourth image. Once the dataset is prepared, neural network mapping can be constructed as shown in subplots a, b, and c of Figure 2. Although rocks are inherently three-dimensional and more suited for 3D super-resolution, our choice of a 2D approach was carefully considered. This method maintains accuracy while offering significant computational advantages: computational complexity reduces from $O(n^3)$ to $O(n^2)$, greatly improving processing efficiency; it can handle larger XY planes and better characterize spatial heterogeneity, allowing us to extend our research from micro-nanometer scale to centimeter or decimeter core scales. Due to the spatial invariance of convolution-based neural networks, we can further divide images into smaller sub-images for training, and during inference, with batch size=1, larger images can be supported as input.!![image2](./figures/FigS2.png)
Appendix 1 shows the visual numerical indicators and visualization comparisons of the MRCCM dataset across different models. While bicubic interpolation increases resolution, image quality improvement is very limited. For grayscale digital cores, we prefer results closer to 0 (pores) or 255 (matrix), rather than ambiguous results (gray). As this dataset is relatively simple, we tested various common deep learning networks with excellent results. This experiment validated our hypothesis - reconstructing high-resolution global images from low-resolution global images.
## PART2
![image2](./figures/Fig3.png)
As mentioned in the background, even when high-resolution images are scaled down to their resolution limit, blurry results appear. Due to resolution limitations, CT cannot determine whether a pixel belongs to pore space or matrix, resulting in values between 0 and 255, leading to ambiguous results. This sparked our curiosity to further study higher resolution images. We collaborated with Shenzhen University using microCT's current resolution limit of 1μm/pixel to scan a more challenging dataset. To study image reconstruction across two resolution levels, we scanned at three resolutions: 16, 4, and 1μm/pixel. We attempted to train a 16-4 μm/pixel mapping model, then used 4μm/pixel images as input to predict unseen cases. Machine learning models often perform poorly when predicting features not present in the dataset. However, for porous media, characteristics we care about like porosity, tortuosity, and Euler number are directly related to resolution. We believe that even without seeing these in the dataset, the model would preserve these topological feature mappings when trained on 16-4μm/pixel data. We focus less on whether the image style matches ground truth and more on topological and physical indicators affecting digital cores. Dataset visualization through Figure 1's c,d shows that 16-4 micron image mapping is more complex, not simply black and white like MRCCM, with many gray areas even at high resolution. At 4μm/pixel, specific classification remains unclear, which we attribute to carbonate rocks' unique, more complex micro-nano pores, unobservable through microCT, increasing model convergence difficulty.

The super-resolution reconstruction results can be seen in Figure 3. Our proposed new model SwinIRGAN (combining Swin Transformer and GAN) performed exceptionally well, adapting to more complex situations. Convolution-based EDSR and ESRGAN showed varying degrees of artifacts (periodically appearing small patterns) typical of super-resolution model convergence failure. Not just in visual metrics, but our model based on SwinIR better predicted parameters like porosity, Euler number, and specific surface area. SwinIR shows some artifacts due to lack of GAN loss constraints. This experiment, combined with MRCCM dataset validation, proves GAN loss effectively helps reconstruct high-resolution features and suppress artifacts. So far, we've discussed existing research using paired datasets for training and prediction.
![image2](./figures/Fig4.png)
Having established that models can increase image resolution by 4x, we tested model performance on never-before-seen data by inputting 4μm/pixel images into the previously trained 16-4μm model to predict 1μm/pixel images. Since the output never appeared in the training set, visual metrics would be poor for any model, so we focused on topology-related physical indicators: porosity, Euler number, and specific surface area. Topological indicators change with resolution, so perhaps the underlying topological property change patterns learned from 16-4μm could transfer to accurately predicting 1μm/pixel images. As shown in Figure 4, our strongest model SwinIRGAN could effectively predict relevant physical indicators with controlled error margins.
![image2](./figures/Fig5.png)
In practical applications, we typically can only obtain images at one resolution; in-situ scanning of the same core twice is rare. Thus, we designed a final experiment using the MRCCM dataset. With two resolution levels from in-situ scanning, we considered super-resolving low-resolution 3D images twice, each time increasing resolution 4x, expanding data volume to $4^3 \times 4^3$ - 4096 times the original. While cross-scale study is desirable, too large a scale makes digital core loading difficult on typical computer memory. Furthermore, even if we accurately predicted higher resolution features, convincing engineers to adopt our model results would be difficult without ground truth. Thus, we adopted a downsampling strategy for highest-resolution images lacking ground truth reference, specifically: train super-resolution models on paired datasets, pass low-resolution models through the super-resolution model twice to obtain images beyond current instrumental resolution, then downsample these images to match current maximum instrumental resolution, as shown in Figure 5. Visually, differences become indistinguishable, but physical indicators reveal interesting findings: predicted porosity largely matches ground truth, Euler number is about 15% larger on logarithmic scale (roughly an order of magnitude), and specific surface area is about 2% larger on logarithmic scale. This suggests super-resolution models can preserve higher resolution features during downsampling while maintaining resolution, potentially improving image quality without higher resolution instruments. Finally, a simple question: how can we replicate this experiment and create paired datasets for supervised learning when we typically only have images at one resolution?

The answer is to scan the dataset once at the current instrument's resolution limit as ground truth, then obtain low-resolution images by downsampling the ground truth.
